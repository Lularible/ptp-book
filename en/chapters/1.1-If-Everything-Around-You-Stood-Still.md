# Part 1: It All Begins with Setting the Watch

## 1.1 If Everything Around You Stood Still

### A thought experiment

Close your eyes and count to ten, then open them.

You knew ten seconds had passed. Maybe you counted, maybe you felt your pulse, maybe you just had a vague sense that it was time to look. All three are forms of change. Remove every change and you lose any way to know that time passed at all.

Push that to its limit. Picture a busy intersection at noon, then stop everything at once. A cyclist freezes mid-pedal, one foot above the curb. Fountain spray hangs in the air. A leaf stays suspended at knee height. You alone can still walk and think, so time still passes for you. Now freeze yourself too. Your heart stops, your blood stops, the photoreceptors in your retina stop firing. Consciousness is neural activity, so it stops with them. No sound, no light, no change in temperature, since temperature is molecular motion as well.

Is time still passing?

There is no textbook answer. Mine is that it is not. We have no organ that senses time directly, no needle marked "time" turning in front of our eyes. We infer time from change: the sun moved, so two hours passed; the hourglass emptied, so a minute passed; the digits on a watch ticked, so a second passed. No change means no evidence, and without evidence time is a concept, not something we perceive.

### Augustine got there first

This sounds like the setup to a science fiction film, but people have been arguing about what time is for a long time. In the fourth century, Augustine of Hippo wrote:

> "What then is time? If no one asks me, I know what it is. If I wish to explain it to him who asks, I do not know."

We all feel that time flows, yet the moment we try to define it, it slips away. Augustine pushed the point further. Strictly speaking, only three kinds of time exist: past, present, and future. The past no longer exists. The future does not yet exist. The present is an infinitely short instant that becomes the past the moment you try to grasp it. So time, strictly speaking, is made of things that do not exist, which is absurd and impossible to deny at the same time.

That contradiction is why time is worth thinking about. We cannot define it directly, but we can grasp it indirectly through change. Time is a scale abstracted from change, and it cannot be measured on its own. You cannot hold a ruler against "one hour." You can only measure how far the sun moved, how many times a pendulum swung, how many times a quartz crystal oscillated, and then agree to call that amount of change an hour. Without change there is no time. Change carries time; time measures change.

### Einstein made it worse

Thirteen centuries after Augustine, a patent clerk named Einstein gave an answer that is harder to accept. Special relativity shows that time is not absolute: the same event takes different amounts of time for observers in different states of motion. Einstein reportedly summarized it with a joke. Sit with a nice girl for two hours and it feels like two minutes; sit on a hot stove for two minutes and it feels like two hours. That's relativity. The line is attributed to him and its source is debatable, and it is not rigorous, but the core is right: time depends on the observer's state of motion rather than flowing uniformly in the background.

Einstein also showed that time and space are not independent. They form a single four-dimensional continuum, spacetime. The "now" of everyday life turns out to be an illusion. You may think two events happened at the same time, but an observer moving quickly relative to you may disagree. Simultaneity is relative, not absolute.

This matters for everything that follows. PTP exists to give separate devices a shared sense of "now." If "now" itself is relative, how is that possible? On a small enough scale, within one frame of reference, we can treat "now" as approximately real. PTP does not challenge relativity. It works as close to the limit as relativity allows, achieving nanosecond synchronization within the bounded space and speeds of a network.

### Pinning time to change

Back to practical ground. If we can only measure change, the obvious question is whether we can find a good change to use as a ruler for time. That is what clocks have always been, and each time we swapped the ruler, something in civilization moved with it.

The first ruler was the sky. The sun rises and sets, the moon waxes and wanes, the stars shift across the night. Day, month, and year all come from these changes, and we still use them because they are stable and universal.

The problem with the sky is that it is coarse. It tells you roughly what time of day it is, not the exact hour and minute. So people built their own regular motion: water clocks, hourglasses, pendulums, spring-driven movements. Water dripping through a small hole drops at a roughly uniform rate. A pendulum takes a roughly equal time per swing. Both are rulers, and both drift with wear, temperature, and gravity.

Physics gave a better one. A quartz watch drives a crystal through the piezoelectric effect at a resonant frequency, usually 32,768 vibrations per second. Quartz is stable but not immune to temperature, which is why precision work uses temperature-compensated (TCXO) or oven-controlled (OCXO) oscillators. Clocks based on quartz lose less than a second a day.

That is still not enough for communications, financial trading, or research, so we went to the atom. The energy-level transitions inside an atom emit radiation at a frequency set by physical constants, which by theory never drifts. In 1967 the General Conference on Weights and Measures redefined the second as the duration of 9,192,631,770 periods of the radiation between the two hyperfine levels of the ground state of cesium-133. Nothing in that definition is man-made. Measure the same transition on Earth, on Mars, or in another galaxy, and you get the same second. It is the most stable change we have found.

### The conductor and the orchestra

One analogy will come back repeatedly.

A symphony orchestra keeps time through a conductor. The musicians watch the baton and listen to each other. If everyone follows the beat, the music holds together. If one player rushes half a beat ahead or lags half a beat behind, the whole thing falls apart.

The conductor is the grandmaster clock, the source of time. The musicians are slave clocks that must stay synchronized to it. The beats are the time scale, and the musicians listening to each other are the protocol interaction. An orchestra where each player keeps his own reference dissolves into noise. A good one holds a single reference, maintained by constant small corrections.

PTP does the same thing to a network. It installs a virtual conductor and has every device follow it to within nanoseconds.

### One thing to try

Find any clock with a seconds display: a phone, a computer screen, a wall clock. Watch the seconds.

The instant it goes from 59 to 00, say "now" out loud.

Three things are worth noticing. First, when you said "now," did you mean the moment the digit changed or the moment your mouth moved? Time passed between them. Second, light takes time to travel from the screen to your eyes, so the instant you saw the change is not the instant it happened. The delay is tiny, but it is not zero. Third, ignoring light entirely, the moment "00" appears on the screen is not the moment the device's internal logic decided the hour had turned over. The display refresh and the rendering have latency of their own.

All three point at one fact: the simultaneity we take for granted is never truly simultaneous. We only treat events as simultaneous because our scale is coarse. PTP pushes that approximation down from seconds to milliseconds, microseconds, and nanoseconds.

This section asked one question, what time actually is, and it left us with four things. Time does not stand on its own but is carried by change. Keeping time has always meant hunting for a stable change to use as a ruler. Simultaneity is only an approximation. And the goal of PTP is to make separate devices agree on "now" as closely as possible.

Next we leave the thought experiments. In 1707 a British fleet ran onto rocks in fog, four ships and nearly two thousand men lost; afterward, how to know accurate time, and your longitude, at sea became a problem Britain could not put down. Navigation, railways, and power grids all paid for the inability to keep time, and each solved it in turn.
