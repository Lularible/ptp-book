# Part 2: Deep Dive into the PTP Protocol

## 2.1 When the Conductor Steps into the Concert Hall: The Three Roles in a PTP Network

### What roles does a symphony performance need?

The symphony-orchestra analogy from Part 1 returns here.

We can now step into this "concert hall" (the PTP network).

In Part 1 we already met the conductor, the grandmaster clock, the source of time. But beyond the conductor, a symphony performance needs three further roles.

First, the musicians. They sit across the stage, holding different instruments. They watch the conductor's gestures and adjust their own playing tempo.

Second, the section conductors. When an orchestra is large and the musicians are spread out, each section gets its own conductor, who watches the chief's beat and relays it to the musicians in his section.

Finally, there is an easily overlooked role: the stage manager. He isn't in the spotlight; he works behind the scenes, passing messages: "The third movement starts in 30 seconds," "The conductor needs to speed up." He doesn't change the music's tempo, but he makes sure all information reaches every musician accurately and on time.

### The three "performance roles" in a PTP network

A PTP network has exactly three clock types, each matching a role in the concert hall.

#### Ordinary Clock: the musician on stage

Definition: a PTP instance with exactly one PTP port.

An ordinary clock is like a musician on stage. It has one "window" to the outside world, a single PTP port. Through that port it can receive the conductor's instructions, or send signals to other musicians.

An ordinary clock has two states:
- MASTER state: it considers itself accurate enough to act as a "deputy conductor," sending time signals to other devices.
- SLAVE state: it acknowledges it's not accurate enough and needs to follow someone else.

Typical examples: your computer's NIC, your 5G base station, your industrial controller. As long as a device only needs to synchronize time on a single network interface, it is an ordinary clock.

Key features:
- Number of ports: 1
- Clock state: MASTER or SLAVE
- Typical applications: end devices, edge-of-network devices

#### Boundary Clock: the "section conductor" connecting regions

Definition: a PTP instance with multiple PTP ports.

A boundary clock is like the "section conductor" of a large concert hall. Imagine an enormous stage divided into three sections: strings, brass, and percussion. Each section has its own conductor, who watches the conductor-in-chief and relays the beat to the musicians in his section.

The boundary clock's core roles:
1. Isolate error: a problem in one section doesn't propagate to others.
2. Extend the network: it can connect more devices.
3. Hierarchical synchronization: it forms a clear synchronization tree.

How a boundary clock works:

Suppose a boundary clock has three ports: A, B, and C.

Port A is near the master clock, in the SLAVE state, receiving the master's time signal.

Ports B and C are far from the master, in the MASTER state, sending time signals to downstream devices.

Internally, the boundary clock does this:
1. Port A receives the master's Sync message.
2. The boundary clock adjusts its own Local PTP Clock.
3. Ports B and C send new Sync messages based on this adjusted local clock.

Key point: a boundary clock *terminates* PTP messages and *regenerates* new ones. Messages are processed and regenerated inside the boundary clock; they do not pass through it unchanged.

The benefit is error isolation.

Suppose there were no boundary clocks, and a message had to traverse 10 switches from master to slave. Each switch introduces delay jitter (processing delay is nondeterministic). Stack ten switches' jitter on top of each other, and synchronization accuracy collapses.

With a boundary clock, jitter is "zeroed out" at each hop. The boundary clock disciplines its own clock against the upstream signal, then sends messages downstream at a stable rate.

Key features:
- Number of ports: 2 or more
- Clock states: some ports in SLAVE, some in MASTER
- Typical applications: network switches, routers, time distributors

#### Transparent Clock: the honest stage manager

Definition: measures how long PTP event messages take to pass through the instance, and provides that information to the PTP instances receiving those messages.

A transparent clock is like the stage manager. He stands behind the scenes, doesn't participate in the performance, but he does one crucial thing: records how long messages take to pass through.

Suppose the conductor raises his hand at second 0. That signal passes through the stage manager to the percussionist at the back. The stage manager records:
- "I received the conductor's signal at second 0.001."
- "I passed the signal to the percussionist at second 0.002."
- "The signal stayed with me for 0.001 seconds."

A transparent clock does exactly the same.

When a PTP message passes through a transparent clock, the TC:
1. Records the ingress timestamp.
2. Records the egress timestamp.
3. Computes the residence time = egress − ingress.
4. Writes the residence time into the message's correctionField.

In this way, downstream devices learn exactly how long the message was held along its path.

Key characteristics of a transparent clock:

It does not adjust its own clock. Its Local Clock free-runs; it need not synchronize with anyone.

It does not change the message's source or destination addresses. A message passes through it like light through air, it merely gains a "tardiness record."

It does not participate in the BMCA (Best Master Clock Algorithm). It doesn't care who the master is; it only faithfully records time.

Two types of transparent clocks:

End-to-End Transparent Clock (E2E TC):
- Forwards Delay_Req / Delay_Resp messages.
- Measures only residence time, not link delay.
- The slave is responsible for measuring the delay over the whole path.

Peer-to-Peer Transparent Clock (P2P TC):
- Drops Delay_Req / Delay_Resp messages.
- Measures both residence time *and* link delay.
- Uses Pdelay_Req / Pdelay_Resp to measure each link.

Why does a P2P TC drop Delay_Req/Delay_Resp? Because the P2P mechanism measures link by link: every transparent clock already knows the delay of the links on both sides of it. When it forwards a Sync, it writes "residence time + ingress link delay" into the correctionField. Downstream devices then have no need to send Delay_Req to measure the path delay.

Key features:
- Clock state: no state machine, doesn't participate in BMCA
- Clock synchronization: not required
- Typical applications: network switches, routers (to improve accuracy)

#### Ordinary vs. Boundary vs. Transparent Clock: the differences in one table

| Feature | Ordinary Clock | Boundary Clock | Transparent Clock |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| **PTP ports** | 1 | ≥2 | ≥1 (usually several) |
| **Clock adjustment** | Yes (follows the master) | Yes (follows the master) | No (free-runs) |
| **Participates in BMCA** | Yes | Yes | No |
| **Message handling** | Terminates / initiates | Terminates / initiates / regenerates | Passes through (adds timestamps) |
| **State machine** | Yes (nine states) | Independent state machine per port | None |
| **Typical device** | End devices, base stations | PTP switches | Ordinary switches |
| **Effect on sync accuracy** | Depends on local clock quality | Can isolate error accumulation | Eliminates jitter and residence delay |
| **Cost** | Low | Medium-high | Medium |

### Domain: the private boxes of the concert hall

A PTP network has another important concept: the domain.

Imagine a large concert hall with several private boxes. Each box is giving a performance, but they play different pieces. Box A plays Beethoven, Box B plays Mozart. They don't interfere with each other, because they're in different spaces.

A PTP domain is such a "private box."

What domains do:

1. Isolation: PTP instances in different domains don't interfere with each other.
2. Multiple time scales: different domains can use different time references.
3. Multiple profiles: different domains can follow different configuration rules.

How domains are identified:

The header of every PTP message carries two domain-identification fields:
- domainNumber: 0-255 (user configurable)
- sdoId: a 12-bit identifier (for isolating profiles defined by different standards organizations)

When a PTP instance receives a message, it checks:
- Is the message's domainNumber the same as mine?
- Is the message's sdoId the same as mine?

If not, it silently drops the message.

Domains matter in three situations.

Scenario 1: one network, many applications

A factory has two production lines. Line A uses PTP to coordinate robots, requiring 1 microsecond accuracy. Line B uses PTP to monitor equipment status, requiring 1 millisecond accuracy.

Both lines' PTP devices sit on the same physical network, so they must be kept from interfering.

The solution: use different domains. Line A uses domainNumber=0, Line B uses domainNumber=1. Their PTP messages are unrelated, and each runs its own synchronization.

Scenario 2: redundant backup

A power system has two PTP networks: the primary and the backup. Physically independent, but both must ultimately synchronize to the same time reference.

If they use the same domainNumber, what happens? BMCA could elect a device in the primary network to be the master of the backup network, causing cross-synchronization and violating the redundancy design.

With different domainNumbers, the two networks are fully independent.

Scenario 3: profiles from different standards organizations

The IEEE 802.1 Working Group defines the 802.1AS profile (for audio-video bridging). ITU-T defines the G.8275.1 profile (for telecommunications). The two profiles have different requirements and different defaults.

If they used the same domainNumber, devices of the two profiles could interfere.

With different sdoIds (IEEE 802.1 uses 0x100, ITU-T uses a value in 0x300-0xFFC), profile-level isolation is achieved.

### Timescale: the "beat" of the performance

The domain defines "which private box"; the timescale defines "which beat you play in."

PTP supports two timescales:

#### The PTP Timescale

Definition: uses the second as defined by International Atomic Time (TAI), with the epoch at 1 January 1970 00:00:00 TAI.

Characteristics:
- Based on physical constants, accurate in theory, forever.
- Relationship to UTC: PTP time = UTC time + accumulated leap seconds.
- As of 2026, PTP time is 37 seconds ahead of UTC (i.e., UTC = PTP − 37).

Typical applications: those needing traceability to international standard time (finance, power, communications).

#### The ARB (Arbitrary) Timescale

Definition: the epoch is set by an administrative process; the second is defined by the master clock.

Characteristics:
- No requirement to align with any standard time.
- The master can say "the time is now 0," and every slave follows from zero.
- Usually used in closed systems that need not synchronize with external time.

Typical applications: industrial control, laboratory testing, closed networks.

The key differences:

| Feature | PTP Timescale | ARB Timescale |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Epoch** | Fixed (1970-01-01 00:00:00 TAI) | Set administratively |
| **Second length** | TAI second (SI second) | Defined by the master |
| **UTC relationship** | Computable (needs leap-second info) | Not applicable |
| **Traceability** | Traceable to international standards | Not traceable |
| **Typical application** | Communications, finance, power | Industrial, laboratory |

### Port state: the musician's "performance state"

A PTP port has nine states, like a musician's different performance states.

#### The state table

| State | English | Description | What messages can it send? |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| **Initializing** | INITIALIZING | Initializing, not yet ready | No messages |
| **Fault** | FAULTY | Something broke | Only responds to management |
| **Disabled** | DISABLED | Manually disabled | No messages |
| **Listening** | LISTENING | Waiting for Announce messages | May send Pdelay messages |
| **Pre-Master** | PRE_MASTER | Preparing to be master, still waiting | No timing messages |
| **Master** | MASTER | Already the master, sending time signals | Sends all messages |
| **Passive** | PASSIVE | Passive, not participating in sync | Only sends Pdelay messages |
| **Uncalibrated** | UNCALIBRATED | Calibrating, not yet stable | May send Delay_Req |
| **Slave** | SLAVE | Synchronized to the master | Sends Delay_Req |

#### A typical state transition flow

Scenario: an ordinary clock powering up

```
Power on
  ↓
INITIALIZING (initialize data sets, check hardware)
  ↓
LISTENING (listen for Announce messages)
  ↓
  ├── Received a better master's Announce?
  │     ↓
  │   UNCALIBRATED (start synchronizing)
  │     ↓
  │   SLAVE (sync complete)
  │
  ├── Timeout, no Announce received?
  │     ↓
  │   MASTER (take over as the master clock directly)
  │
  └── BMCA decision: am I the best?
        ↓
      PRE_MASTER (wait for confirmation)
        ↓
      MASTER (become the master clock)
```

One state deserves extra attention.

PASSIVE is the most misunderstood state. Its purpose is "pruning", preventing synchronization loops.

Imagine a triangular topology: A-B-C-A.

If A is the master and B and C are both slaves, B syncs from A and C syncs from A. No problem.

But B and C might also establish a master-slave relationship.

Case 1: B becomes C's master. But B is itself a slave whose time comes from A. C then indirectly syncs from A, just one more hop up, which looks harmless at first glance.

The problem: if the topology changes, or the BMCA computation errs, a loop can form.

The PTP standard mandates: on any given PTP communication path, there can be only one master. If BMCA determines that a port should neither become a master (because a better slave path already exists) nor become a slave (in the usual sense), that port enters PASSIVE.

A PASSIVE port sends no Sync, receives no Sync; it only sends Pdelay messages (for link delay measurement). It's a "shelved" port, neither participating in synchronization nor disturbing others.

### Entity identity: each musician's "badge"

In a PTP network, every PTP instance has a unique identity: the clockIdentity.

#### The structure of clockIdentity

clockIdentity is an 8-byte (64-bit) identifier. It can be constructed in several ways:

Method 1: derived from a MAC address (EUI-48)

Generated by the EUI-64 rule: split the MAC in half and insert `FF-FE` in the middle:

```
[OUI (3 bytes)] [FF-FE (2 bytes)] [NIC (3 bytes)]
```

Example: MAC = 00-1B-19-00-00-01 → clockIdentity = 00-1B-19-FF-FE-00-00-01

Method 2: based on EUI-64

Use an 8-byte EUI-64 identifier directly.

Method 3: based on other unique identifiers

E.g., generated randomly (linuxptp's default is to generate one from a random seed), as long as global uniqueness is guaranteed.

#### Why is clockIdentity needed?

Scenario 1: BMCA decisions

When several devices could become the master, BMCA compares their priorities. If every attribute is identical, it compares the clockIdentity, the smaller one wins.

Scenario 2: path tracing

When a boundary clock forwards an Announce, it can record the list of clockIdentities it has passed through in a PATH_TRACE TLV. If a boundary clock finds its own clockIdentity already in the list, a loop has formed, and it drops the message.

Scenario 3: fault diagnosis

An administrator can use the clockIdentity to quickly locate a malfunctioning device.

#### portIdentity: each port's own identity

A PTP instance may have several ports (e.g., a boundary clock). Each port needs an independent identity: the portIdentity.

A portIdentity has two parts:
- clockIdentity (8 bytes): identifies the PTP instance.
- portNumber (2 bytes): identifies the port.

Port-number rules:
- Valid range: 1-65534
- 65535 (0xFFFF): "all ports" (used in management messages)
- 0: "invalid" or "uninitialized"

Example:

A boundary clock with clockIdentity 00-1B-19-00-00-00-00-01 has 3 ports. Then:
- Port 1's portIdentity: {clockIdentity: 00-1B-19-00-00-00-00-01, portNumber: 1}
- Port 2's portIdentity: {clockIdentity: 00-1B-19-00-00-00-00-01, portNumber: 2}
- Port 3's portIdentity: {clockIdentity: 00-1B-19-00-00-00-00-01, portNumber: 3}

### Summary: the "performance company" of a PTP network

One table summarizes the roles of a PTP network:

| Role | Analogy | Ports | Synchronizes? | Participates in BMCA? | Typical device |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| **Ordinary Clock** | Musician | 1 | Yes | Yes | End devices |
| **Boundary Clock** | Section conductor | ≥2 | Yes | Yes | PTP switches |
| **Transparent Clock** | Stage manager | ≥1 | No | No | Ordinary switches |

Domains, timescales, port states, and identity together describe the "organizational structure" of a PTP network.

Each role in a PTP network now has a clear function.

The remaining question is who gets to be the conductor.

When several devices are capable of being master (say, each connected to GPS), the network must decide who is the real "conductor".

The next section covers the most elegant algorithm in PTP: the BMCA (Best Master Clock Algorithm), which automatically elects the most suitable master, just as an orchestra automatically picks its best conductor.

Imagine: your network has 10 devices, and all of them have a GPS antenna. In theory, any of them could be master. If they all rush to send Announce messages, each claiming "I am the master," the result is chaos: every device receives multiple Announce messages and doesn't know whom to listen to.

PTP solves this with a "democratic election" mechanism.
