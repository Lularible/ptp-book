# 2.10 More Than Phase Alignment: The Secret of Frequency Synchronization

## Two clocks, two "heartbeats"

Imagine two runners on a track.

Case 1: both run at the same speed, but at different positions.

- Runner A is at the start line.
- Runner B is 10 meters ahead of the start line.

This is a phase offset. The solution: make B step back 10 meters, and they're aligned.

The phase adjustment of the previous section is exactly this "stepping back 10 meters."

Case 2: both are at the same position, but run at different speeds.

- Runner A runs 10 m/s.
- Runner B runs 10.1 m/s.

This is a frequency offset. Even though they're aligned now, B will lead by 1 meter in 10 seconds.

Phase adjustment achieves "side by side now," but it can't easily achieve "side by side forever."

This section covers frequency synchronization: how to adjust a clock's "heartbeat rate."

## What is frequency offset?

Frequency offset: the difference between the slave clock's rate and the master clock's rate.

Symbol: y, or Δf/f

Units: dimensionless (ppm, ppb, etc.)

Analogy: two runners, one at 10 m/s and one at 10.01 m/s. The 0.01 m/s difference is the frequency offset.

Key insight:

A phase offset is the accumulated result of a frequency offset:

```
phase offset = ∫ frequency offset dt
```

Example:

```
frequency offset = +10 ppm (slave runs one hundred-thousandth fast)
after 1000 seconds

phase offset = 10 ppm × 1000 s
            = 10 × 10⁻⁶ × 1000
            = 0.01 s
            = 10 ms
```

Even if phase adjustment aligns the clocks, the frequency offset will separate them again.

## Why is frequency synchronization so important?

### Scenario 1: holdover

Suppose the slave has synchronized to the master, and the phase offset is near zero.

Suddenly the master fails and the slave loses its reference.

If the slave has no frequency synchronization:
- Local crystal frequency offset: +10 ppm
- Error accumulated per second: 10 µs
- Per minute: 600 µs
- Per hour: 36 ms

If the frequency is already synchronized:
- The slave knows its frequency correction: −10 ppm
- Even without the master, it can keep applying this correction
- Error accumulates far more slowly

### Scenario 2: fewer synchronization messages

If the slave's frequency is already synced to the master:
- The frequency offset is near zero.
- The phase offset accumulates slowly.
- The Sync message rate can be reduced.

Benefits:
- Lower network load.
- Lower CPU load.
- Accuracy still maintained.

### Scenario 3: long-term stability

Phase adjustment only aligns the clocks temporarily.

Without frequency synchronization:
- Adjust every few seconds.
- The clock keeps jumping.
- The system is unstable.

With frequency synchronization:
- The phase offset accumulates slowly.
- Adjustments become less frequent.
- Long-term stability.

## Frequency synchronization in PTP

### Method 1: deriving the frequency offset from phase offsets

Principle: measure the phase offset several times and compute its rate of change.

Formula:

```
frequency offset ≈ (offsetₙ − offsetₙ₋₁) / (Tₙ − Tₙ₋₁)
```

Example:

```
T₁ = 1000 s, offset₁ = +100 ns
T₂ = 1001 s, offset₂ = +110 ns

frequency offset = (110 ns − 100 ns) / (1001 s − 1000 s)
                = 10 ns/s
                = 10 ppb
```

Implementation:

The slave continuously measures offsetFromMaster, using a sliding-window average or a low-pass filter to extract the frequency offset.

Advantages:
- No extra messages.
- Simple to implement.

Disadvantages:
- Needs multiple measurements; slow to converge.
- Affected by network jitter.

### Method 2: direct frequency measurement

Principle: directly compare the master's and slave's rates.

What the Sync carries:
- originTimestamp: the Sync's send time.
- correctionField: various corrections.

The slave's handling:

1. Receive the Sync, record receive time t2.
2. Compute the corrected master send time:
   ```
   correctedMasterTime = originTimestamp + correctionField + meanDelay
   ```
3. Record the correctedMasterTime and local time for two Syncs.
4. Compute the frequency ratio:
   ```
   frequencyRatio = local-time interval / master-time interval
   ```

Example:

```
Sync 1:
- Local receive time: t₂₁ = 1000.000000100 s
- Corrected master send time: T₁ = 1000.000000000 s

Sync 2:
- Local receive time: t₂₂ = 1001.000000110 s
- Corrected master send time: T₂ = 1001.000000000 s

Local interval = t₂₂ − t₂₁ = 1.000000010 s
Master interval = T₂ − T₁ = 1.000000000 s

frequency ratio = 1.000000010 / 1.000000000 = 1.000000010
frequency offset = ratio − 1 = 10 ppb
```

Advantages:
- High accuracy.
- Fast convergence.

Disadvantages:
- Needs an accurate meanDelay measurement.

## Frequency compensation: adjusting the clock rate

Once the frequency offset is known, the clock rate must be adjusted.

### The basic method

Goal: make the slave's rate equal to the master's.

Formula:

```
adjusted clock rate = original rate × (1 − frequency offset)
```

Example:

```
frequency offset = +10 ppm (slave is fast)
adjusted rate = original × (1 − 0.00001) = original × 0.99999
```

Make the clock run a little slower, aligning it with the master.

### Software implementation

Method: keep a frequency-compensation value and apply it every time the local clock is read.

```c
double frequency_adjustment = 1.0 - frequency_offset;

Timestamp get_adjusted_time() {
    Timestamp raw_time = read_hardware_counter();
    return raw_time * frequency_adjustment + phase_offset;
}
```

### Hardware implementation

Method 1: numerically-controlled oscillator (NCO)

Digitally steer the oscillator frequency.

```
NCO output frequency = reference frequency × (control word / 2^N)
```

Resolution:

For a 32-bit NCO, the frequency step is roughly:

```
step = 125 MHz / 2^32 ≈ 0.03 Hz ≈ 0.24 ppb
```

Method 2: phase-locked loop (PLL)

If the master provides a 1PPS signal, the slave can lock a PLL to it, achieving frequency synchronization.

## Slew adjustment: eliminating a phase offset through rate

Step adjustment (directly modifying the clock value) causes jumps or backward movement.

Slew adjustment is smoother: it eliminates the phase offset gradually by adjusting the clock rate.

### The principle

Suppose offsetFromMaster = −1 ms (the slave is 1 ms behind).

The adjustment works like this:
- Over a period (say, 1 second)
- Let the local clock run slightly fast
- Gradually "catch up" to the master

Mathematical description:

```
during the adjustment, clock rate = normal rate × (1 − offsetFromMaster / adjustment time)
```

Example:

```
offsetFromMaster = −1 ms (slave is 1 ms behind)
adjustment time = 1 s

Speed up by = 1 ms / 1 s = +1000 ppm

Over 1 s the clock runs 1000 ppm fast:
- Normal would advance: 1 s
- Actual advance: 1 s × (1 + 0.001) = 1.001 s
- That's "catching up" 1 ms

After 1 s, the phase offset is gone; the clock returns to normal rate
```

### Difference from frequency compensation

| Feature | Slew adjustment | Frequency compensation |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Goal** | Eliminate a phase offset | Eliminate a frequency offset |
| **Duration** | Temporary (restores after done) | Continuous (applied long-term) |
| **Magnitude** | Larger (can reach thousands of ppm) | Smaller (usually tens of ppm) |
| **Trigger** | Phase offset exceeds a threshold | Frequency offset is measured |

### Advantages

- Time remains continuous, no jumps, no backward movement.
- The application layer never notices.
- Smoother than a step adjustment.

### Disadvantages

- The clock is less accurate during the adjustment.
- Takes longer to complete.
- Limited magnitude (too large would wreck short-term accuracy).

## The clock servo: dynamic closed-loop control

### What is a clock servo?

Servo: an automatic control system that continuously adjusts its output through feedback to track a target value.

Clock servo: a control system that dynamically adjusts a local clock's phase and frequency to track the master.

### Why do we need a servo?

Both the phase and frequency offsets keep changing:

- Network delay jitter affects phase measurements.
- Temperature changes affect crystal frequency.
- Other environmental factors.

Static correction values can't handle these. Dynamic adjustment is required.

### The basic structure of a servo

```
                ┌─────────────┐
master time ---->│ comparator  │<------ local clock
                │             │
                │ compute error│
                │ (offset)    │
                └──────┬──────┘
                       │
                       ▼
                ┌─────────────┐
                │  controller │
                │  (PI)       │
                └──────┬──────┘
                       │
                       ▼
                ┌─────────────┐
                │  actuator   │
                │ (adjust)    │
                └──────┬──────┘
                       │
                       └──────────> phase + frequency adjustment
```

### The PI controller

The PTP clock servo typically uses a PI controller (proportional-integral).

Formula:

```
control output = Kp × offset + Ki × ∫offset dt
```

Where:
- Kp: the proportional gain.
- Ki: the integral gain.
- offset: the current phase offset.
- ∫offset dt: the integral of the phase offset (accumulated error).

The proportional (P) term:

```
Kp × offset
```

- Responds to the current phase offset.
- Produces a phase adjustment (removes the current offset).

The integral (I) term:

```
Ki × ∫offset dt
```

- Responds to the accumulated phase offset.
- Produces a frequency adjustment (removes long-term accumulation).

### Why does the integral term correspond to a frequency adjustment?

The integral of the phase offset grows when the frequency offset is nonzero:

```
∫offset dt ≈ frequency offset × time
```

The larger the frequency offset, the faster the integral grows. The I term responds to this accumulation by adjusting the clock rate.

So:
- P term → phase adjustment (fixes "now").
- I term → frequency adjustment (fixes "the future").

### Why not use the D term?

A PID controller also has a derivative (D) term:

```
Kd × d(offset)/dt
```

But the PTP clock servo typically omits the D term, for two reasons:

1. offset itself is noisy: measurement errors, network jitter, etc.
2. the derivative amplifies noise: it makes the control output unstable.

### Tuning the PI parameters

Choosing the PI parameters is the heart of servo design.

Parameters too aggressive (large Kp, Ki):
- Fast response.
- But prone to oscillation and instability.

Parameters too conservative (small Kp, Ki):
- Stable.
- But slow response and long convergence.

Rules of thumb:

| Network characteristics | Kp | Ki | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Stable (low jitter) | large | large | can adjust aggressively |
| Unstable (high jitter) | small | small | must adjust conservatively |

## A complete frequency-synchronization example

### Scenario

- Master clock frequency: an exact 125 MHz.
- Slave local crystal: nominally 125 MHz, actually 125.00125 MHz (+10 ppm).
- Sync interval: 1 second.

### The initial phase

The 1st Sync:

- Master send time: T₁ = 0 s
- Slave receive time: t₂₁ = 0 s (assume initially phase-aligned)

The 2nd Sync:

- Master send time: T₂ = 1 s
- Slave receive time: t₂₂ = 1.00001 s (crystal 10 ppm fast)

Measure the phase offset:

```
offset = 1.00001 − 1 = 10 µs
```

Compute the frequency offset:

```
frequency offset = offset / interval = 10 µs / 1 s = 10 ppm
```

### Applying frequency compensation

The PI control output:

- P term: produces a phase adjustment (possibly a slew of 10 µs).
- I term: produces a frequency adjustment = −10 ppm.

After applying the compensation:

```
adjusted clock rate = original rate × (1 − 0.00001) = original × 0.99999
```

The 3rd Sync:

The master advances 1 second; the slave's raw time advances 1.00001 s.

After adjustment:

```
slave time = 1.00001 × 0.99999 = 1.00000 s
```

Matching the master! Frequency synchronization succeeds.

### The steady-state phase

The 10th Sync:

- offset ≈ 0
- frequency offset ≈ 0
- PI output ≈ 0

The clock stays stable.

Suppose temperature changes and the crystal drifts to +11 ppm:

- offset begins to accumulate.
- The PI controller detects the change.
- It adjusts the frequency compensation.

Continuous dynamic adjustment keeps long-term synchronization.

## Summary: the core points of frequency synchronization

Frequency offset:
- A difference in clock rates.
- The cause of phase-offset accumulation.

Why it matters:
- Long-term stability.
- Holdover support.
- Fewer synchronization messages.

Frequency measurement methods:
- Deriving it from phase offsets.
- Direct frequency measurement.

Frequency compensation:
- Software: mathematical adjustment.
- Hardware: NCO, PLL.

Slew adjustment:
- Eliminating a phase offset through rate.
- Smooth, no jumps.

The clock servo:
- A PI controller adjusting dynamically.
- P term → phase adjustment.
- I term → frequency adjustment.

Both frequency synchronization and phase synchronization rest on one key prerequisite: accurate timestamps.

Section 2.11 covers hardware timestamps, the true source of PTP's nanosecond precision.

Software and hardware timestamps differ in accuracy by more than 1,000×.

- Software timestamp accuracy: microsecond level (affected by interrupt and scheduling latency).
- Hardware timestamp accuracy: nanosecond level (recorded right at the PHY).

This gap determines whether PTP can reach nanosecond-level synchronization.
