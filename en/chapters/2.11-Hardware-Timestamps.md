# 2.11 Where Do Nanoseconds Come From: The Secret of Hardware Timestamps

## A deceptively simple question: what time is it?

Suppose you want to record "now."

**Method 1: look at your watch**

You look down at your watch. It reads 10:30:45.

Is that "now"?

No. By the time you look down, focus your eyes, and your brain reads the digits, a few hundred milliseconds have gone by.

**Method 2: look at the computer screen**

You press a key. The program calls `gettimeofday()`, which returns a timestamp.

Is that "now"?

Not that either. The call goes out, the kernel handles it, and it comes back to userspace. A few microseconds pass.

**Method 3: hardware timestamping**

At the network connector there's a hardware circuit. The instant a frame passes, it records the exact time.

That's probably the closest you'll get to "now."

PTP's nanosecond precision depends on exactly this kind of **hardware timestamping**.

---

## The timestamp hierarchy: from software to hardware

### Level 1: application-layer timestamps

**Where it lives**: in the application

**How it works**:
1. The frame reaches the NIC.
2. The NIC raises an interrupt.
3. The OS schedules the interrupt handler.
4. The handler copies the frame into a kernel buffer.
5. The application calls `recv()`.
6. The application reads the time.

**Where the delay comes from**:
- Interrupt latency: a few microseconds to tens of microseconds
- Scheduling latency: tens to hundreds of microseconds
- Data copy: a few microseconds

**Total delay**: a few hundred microseconds to 1 ms

**Jitter**: large, because scheduling is unpredictable

**Where this applies**: NTP, ordinary applications

**Accuracy**: millisecond level

---

### Level 2: kernel timestamps

**Where it lives**: the OS kernel

**How it works**:
1. The frame reaches the NIC.
2. The NIC raises an interrupt.
3. The kernel's interrupt handler records the time.
4. The frame travels up to the application layer.

**Where the delay comes from**:
- Interrupt latency: a few microseconds to tens of microseconds
- Interrupt handling: a few microseconds
- Protocol stack, socket queue, and process wakeup: tens of microseconds, varying by up to hundreds

**Total delay**: tens to hundreds of microseconds

**Jitter**: medium

**Where this applies**: low-accuracy PTP

**Accuracy**: microsecond level

---

### Level 3: driver-layer timestamps

**Where it lives**: the NIC driver running on the host CPU (**not in the NIC chip**)

**How it works**:
1. The frame reaches the NIC, and the NIC's DMA moves it into a kernel buffer.
2. In interrupt or NAPI context the driver picks up the completion descriptor and reads the software timestamp right away.

**Where the delay comes from**:
- Driver processing: a few microseconds

**Total delay**: a few to tens of microseconds

**Jitter**: small

**Where this applies**: medium-accuracy PTP

**Accuracy**: sub-microsecond level

---

### Level 4: hardware timestamps

**Where it lives**: the timestamping engine inside the NIC chip (in the MAC on an integrated controller, in the PHY chip on a dedicated PTP PHY)

**How it works**:
1. The frame passes the **timestamp point** (see the message timestamp point below).
2. The timestamping engine latches the local counter and hands the value to the driver over a side channel.

**Where the delay comes from**:
- Hardware delay between the capture point and the physical wire: a few to tens of nanoseconds

**Total delay**: a few to tens of nanoseconds (a fixed systematic offset, which calibration can absorb through egressLatency / ingressLatency)

**Jitter**: very small

**Where this applies**: high-accuracy PTP

**Accuracy**: nanosecond level

---

## How hardware timestamping works

### Who captures the timestamp: MAC or PHY

**The PHY (physical-layer chip)** is the bottom layer of the Ethernet interface. It handles:
- Signal encoding and decoding
- Serial-to-parallel and parallel-to-serial conversion
- Link-state detection

Compared with an ordinary NIC, a **timestamping engine** (usually in the MAC on an integrated controller, in the PHY chip on a dedicated PTP PHY) does three more things:

- Recognizes PTP event messages through the **negotiated filter**
- Records a timestamp as the frame passes
- Passes the timestamp to the driver

**How it's implemented** (all three are **chip vendors' implementation techniques**; the 1588 text only describes them qualitatively):

**Approach 1: a dedicated register (latch the counter)**

The timestamping engine keeps a real-time counter driven by the local crystal. When it recognizes a PTP event message, it latches the counter value into a register. **This is the most common approach, but no clause in the standard's text requires it.** 1588 only says that timestamping at the physical layer "often draws on a hardware assist circuit."

**Approach 2: marking the message**

The PHY carries timestamp information inside the PTP message (as some IEEE 1588 over IEEE 802.3 implementations do).

**Approach 3: a side channel**

The PHY sends the timestamp to the CPU over a side channel such as SPI or I2C.

### The timestamp capture point

**The PTP standard says**: the timestamp is generated when the **message timestamp point** crosses the **reference plane**.

**The message timestamp point**:

Unless the transport-specific annex says otherwise, this is the default rule **shared by every transport**, and it **binds event messages only**: **the start of the first symbol after the start-of-frame delimiter (SFD)**.

```text
Ethernet frame layout (IEEE 802.3 transport as an example):
[Preamble] [SFD] [Destination MAC] [Source MAC] ... [Payload] [FCS]
                 ↑
                 first symbol after SFD — timestamp capture point
```

**The reference plane**:

The standard only defines it as "the reference plane, marking the **boundary between the PTP Instance and the network**." It **doesn't say which layer it sits at**. In practice it may fall at the PHY, the MAC, or the timestamping engine, depending on where the chip puts the capture point. That's exactly why ingressLatency / egressLatency have to compensate.

### Why this point?

(The standard doesn't explain the choice. The three reasons below are reconstructed from an engineering point of view.)

**Reason 1: determinism**

The SFD is an unambiguous marker, easy to spot.

**Reason 2: repeatability**

Whatever the frame carries, the SFD sits in the same place, so the timestamp is repeatable.

**Reason 3: minimum delay**

The timestamp point hugs the first symbol after the SFD, the earliest moment the frame enters the network. Hardware delay from the capture point to there is the smallest, and so is the jitter.

## The challenges of hardware timestamping

### Challenge 1: timestamp delivery delay

The hardware captures the timestamp in the NIC's timestamping engine (inside the MAC, or inside a dedicated PTP PHY), but the timestamp has to reach the PTP stack in software.

The path:
- Timestamping engine (MAC/PHY) → driver → kernel → userspace

**The problem**: along the way, how does the software know which timestamp belongs to which frame?

The standard raises the problem itself and gives the usual hardware-side answer: "To ensure that the timestamps are associated with the correct PTP message, the hardware assist often captures additional information from the PTP event message that is passed with the timestamp to the PTP code." In other words, the hardware assist grabs extra information from the event message and hands it to the protocol code along with the timestamp.

**Solutions**:

**Method 1: tie the timestamp to the message**

Each message carries a unique identifier (sequenceId, say), and the timestamp carries the same identifier.

**Method 2: guarantee the order**

The hardware guarantees that timestamps and frames arrive in the same order, and the software matches them position by position.

### Challenge 2: the clock source

Hardware timestamping needs a clock source.

**Option 1: the local crystal**

Use the crystal on the NIC (usually 25 MHz or 125 MHz).

**Pros**:
- Independent
- Low cost

**Cons**:
- Inaccurate frequency (tens of ppm off)
- Needs software frequency compensation

**Option 2: an external clock input**

Use an external high-accuracy clock, such as a GPS-disciplined atomic clock.

**Pros**:
- Accurate frequency

**Cons**:
- Needs external equipment
- High cost

**Option 3: Synchronous Ethernet (SyncE)**

Recover the clock from the Ethernet link.

**Pros**:
- Reuses existing infrastructure
- High accuracy

**Cons**:
- Every device on the path must support SyncE
- A link break loses the clock

### Challenge 3: clock resolution

The hardware counter's resolution sets the timestamp resolution.

**Example**:

```text
Crystal frequency: 125 MHz
Counter width: 64 bits

resolution = 1 / 125 MHz = 8 ns
```

**Ways to get finer resolution**:

**Method 1: a faster crystal**

A 1 GHz crystal gets you down to 1 ns.

**Method 2: phase interpolation**

Use a phase detector to measure where inside the crystal period the event landed.

**Method 3: DDMTD**

Digital Dual-Mixer Time Difference reaches sub-picosecond resolution.

## Hardware and software together: timestamp correction

### The real timestamp point is off

Hardware timestamping doesn't always capture at the "ideal position."

**Why**:

- Design limits of the capture-point hardware (PHY/MAC)
- Signal propagation delay
- Internal buffering delay

**Example**:

Ideal: the timestamp is captured at the message timestamp point (the start of the first symbol after the SFD).
Real: a fixed hardware delay separates the capture point from the message timestamp point.

**Result**: every timestamp carries a bias in a fixed direction (opposite signs on receive and transmit), which calibration can compensate.

### How to correct it

The PTP standard provides a correction mechanism:

```
actual timestamp (reference plane) = timestamp handed to PTP ± correction delay
```

**Measuring the correction delay**:

The equipment maker has to measure:
- ingressLatency: the interval from when the message timestamp point crosses the reference plane to the receive timestamp handed to PTP (`\<ingressProvidedTimestamp\>`)
- egressLatency: the interval from the transmit timestamp handed to PTP (`\<egressProvidedTimestamp\>`) to when the message timestamp point crosses the reference plane

These delays can come from:
- The design spec (theoretical values)
- A calibration procedure (measured values)
- Configuration parameters (what the user sets)

### timestampCorrectionPortDS

This is an optional part of the PTP dataset. It stores the timestamp correction parameters:

| Member | Meaning |
|:---|:---|
| ingressLatency | ingress timestamp correction delay |
| egressLatency | egress timestamp correction delay |

**Configuration example**:

```
ingressLatency = 10ns (reference plane → timestamp handed to PTP, receive)
egressLatency = 15ns (timestamp handed to PTP → reference plane, transmit)

actual receive timestamp (reference plane) = timestamp handed to PTP - ingressLatency
actual transmit timestamp (reference plane) = timestamp handed to PTP + egressLatency
```

> The standard's sign convention works like this. On receive, the frame crosses the reference plane before it reaches the timestamp point, so you **subtract** ingressLatency from the timestamp-point time to get reference-plane time. On transmit, the frame passes the timestamp point before the reference plane, so you **add** egressLatency.

## Mainstream hardware timestamping options

### Option 1: Intel I210/I350

**Features**:
- Supports IEEE 1588-2008
- Hardware timestamp accuracy: nanosecond level
- Supports one-step and two-step modes

**How it works**:
- A timestamp unit in the MAC/GMAC (SYSTIM/TXTS/RXTS) captures the timestamp
- It reaches the driver through registers
- The driver exposes an API to the application layer

**Where it's used**:
- Industrial control
- Telecom edge equipment

**Features**:

- Supports SyncE

- Can reach extremely high accuracy

**Where it's used**:
- Research
- High-precision measurement

## A complete timestamping walkthrough

### Transmit path

**Application layer**:
1. Builds the PTP message.
2. Calls `send()`.

**Kernel**:
3. The kernel passes the message to the driver.

**Driver**:
4. The driver writes the message into the NIC's transmit queue.
5. The NIC starts transmitting.

**Hardware timestamping**:
6. The timestamping engine detects the SFD.
7. The engine latches the current counter value: t_tx = 1000.000000000 s.
8. The register holds t_tx.

**Driver (later)**:
9. The driver reads the register and gets t_tx.
10. The driver applies the egressLatency correction:
   ```text
     actual transmit time = t_tx + egressLatency
                          = 1000.000000000 + 15ns
                          = 1000.000000015 s
   ```
11. The driver passes the corrected timestamp to the PTP stack.

**PTP stack**:
12. The stack writes the timestamp into the Follow_Up message.

---

### Receive path

**Hardware timestamping**:
1. The timestamping engine detects the SFD.
2. The engine latches the current counter value: t_rx = 1000.000000100 s.
3. The register holds t_rx.
4. The frame itself continues up the normal receive path (MAC → driver).

**Driver**:
5. The driver receives the frame and reads the timestamp register.
6. The driver applies the ingressLatency correction:
   ```text
   actual receive time = t_rx - ingressLatency
                       = 1000.000000100 - 10ns
                       = 1000.000000090 s
   ```
7. The driver passes the frame and the timestamp to the PTP stack together.

**PTP stack**:
8. The stack uses the corrected time in the offset calculation.

---

## Summary: the core points of hardware timestamping

**Timestamp levels**:
- Application layer: millisecond level
- Kernel layer: microsecond level
- Driver layer: sub-microsecond level
- Hardware layer (the timestamping engine inside the NIC MAC; a dedicated PTP PHY may keep it in the PHY chip): nanosecond level

**How hardware timestamping works**:
- The timestamping engine detects PTP messages.
- It latches the counter at the SFD.
- It passes the value to the driver and the stack.

**Key challenges**:
- Timestamp delivery delay
- Choosing a clock source
- Resolution limits

**Timestamp correction**:
- Measure the ingress/egress delay.
- Configure it through timestampCorrectionPortDS.
- Hardware and software work together.

**Mainstream options**:
- Intel I210/I350: general purpose, medium accuracy
- Broadcom BCM5XXX: high-end switches
- Xilinx ZynqMP: SoC integration
- FPGA: extremely high accuracy
