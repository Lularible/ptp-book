# 2.17 Chasing the Speed of Light: White Rabbit and Sub-Nanosecond Synchronization

## The frontier beyond nanoseconds

Ordinary PTP, with hardware timestamps and good networks, reaches tens of nanoseconds. High-end PTP networks reach single-digit nanoseconds.

White Rabbit (WR) pushes past the nanosecond frontier into picoseconds.

Where does that matter?

- Particle accelerators need triggers and time tags that agree far better than a nanosecond.
- Radio astronomy arrays (VLBI) coherently combine signals sampled at geographically distant sites.
- Large physics experiments and some metrology links need sub-nanosecond phase alignment.

## Two clocks are never the same

Synchronization must fix two independent errors, phase and frequency. PTP handles phase over the network; WR pushes both to the limit:

- Frequency: disciplined at Layer-1 with Synchronous Ethernet (SyncE), the receiver locks its own clock to the incoming bit clock, so every node's oscillator runs at exactly the reference rate.
- Phase: refined by PTP messages plus very precise delay measurement.

Once the frequencies are locked, offsets become constant rather than drifting, and a single phase measurement (plus occasional monitoring) suffices.

## The fiber problem: delay and asymmetry

Time-of-day travels over fiber. Two facts complicate it:

1. Fiber delay is large: light in single-mode fiber travels at roughly 5 ns per meter, so a 10 km link is about 50 µs. Wrong by 1% and you're off by 500 ns.
2. The delay isn't symmetric: sender and receiver use different wavelengths (e.g., 1490 nm vs 1310 nm in 1000BASE-BX10). Dispersion makes the two directions travel at different speeds, so the one-way delay differs going opposite directions.

So WR must measure each link's delay, and its asymmetry, precisely, per link.

## How WR measures a link: PTP + DDMTD

WR uses PTP's peer-to-peer mechanism on each link, but measures the turnaround time with DDMTD (Digital Dual-Mixer Time Difference) phase detectors rather than ordinary timestamps.

DDMTD principle: mix the incoming 125 MHz data/clock against a slightly offset reference clock so that phase differences appear at a slower beat frequency where they can be measured with much finer resolution.

With parameters such as:

```
f_in       = 125 MHz
f_PLL      = 16384/16385 × f_in ≈ 124.992 MHz
beat       ≈ 7.63 kHz
magnification = N+1 = 16385×
input phase resolution = input period / (N+1) = 8 ns / 16385 ≈ 0.49 ps
```

WR's demonstrated accuracy: ±4 ps phase and ±400 fs time-stamp resolution; for long-haul, under 100 ps link-by-link (values quoted for the classic WR spec).

The measured link delay (including asymmetry, calibrated per link) folds into the PTP correction, so every hop knows its one-way delay to picoseconds.

## The role of SyncE

SyncE removes the frequency component from the problem. The physical bit clock carries frequency traceability end to end, so the local oscillators stay syntonized even when PTP messages are interrupted. WR then only needs to measure and fix phase.

This separation, frequency by hardware (SyncE), phase by protocol (PTP + DDMTD), is why WR achieves pico-scale alignment without excessive messaging.

## The White Rabbit ecosystem

- White Rabbit PTP Core: the reference design (a soft-core in FPGA) implementing the WR protocol stack and disciplined timing.
- WR switches and timing nodes: VME/PCIe/standalone nodes with headroom for thousands of km of cascaded links.
- The WR standard: developed by CERN and partners; the protocol specification ("White Rabbit") and the IEEE 1588-2019 High-Accuracy Delay Request-Response Default PTP Profile (Annex I.5, profile 00-1B-19-03-01-00) capture much of it.
- The high-accuracy profile requires the physical-interface timing option (Annex L), external port configuration, and timestamp corrections (16.7) and link asymmetry (16.8), implementing the "high accuracy" path in the standard itself.

## Numbers worth remembering

```
light in fiber:                     ~5 ns per meter
WR phase accuracy:                  ±4 ps (short haul), sub-100 ps long haul
DDMTD resolution gain:              thousands of x
SyncE:                              exact frequency distribution at Layer 1
```

Sub-nanosecond performance is the design point for WR.

## Summary

- PTP fixes phase; WR additionally locks frequency with SyncE and measures phase with picosecond-grade DDMTD.
- Fiber delay and wavelength-dependent asymmetry must be measured per link.
- The high-accuracy profile (I.5) of IEEE 1588-2019 captures these ideas in a standard.

Precision is one virtue; conformance is another. Real deployments need agreed rules: which mechanisms, which defaults, which options.

A profile pins down the options so that products interoperate: message intervals, priorities, transport, optional features.
