# 2.3 Passports and Visas for Time: PTP Domains and Timescales

## A passport for time travel

Imagine you're traveling abroad.

First, you need a passport, proving which country's citizen you are. It carries your nationality, your passport number, and its validity.

Second, you need a visa, allowing you to enter the destination country. The visa stipulates how long you may stay and what you may do.

Finally, you need to mind the time zone. When you fly from Beijing to New York, you set your watch back, otherwise your time and the local time won't line up.

The "international travel" of a PTP network needs a similar set of mechanisms:

- Domain = the passport (which "time country" you're a citizen of)
- Timescale = the time zone (which "time standard" you use)
- Traceability information = the visa (where your time comes from, and whether it's trustworthy)

## Domain: time's "nationality"

### Why do we need domains?

Scenario 1: a smart factory

A smart factory has three production lines:

- Line A: makes chips, devices need nanosecond-level sync, using PTP over Ethernet.
- Line B: assembles automobiles, devices need millisecond-level sync, also PTP over Ethernet.
- Monitoring system: monitors the environment, second-level sync is plenty, using NTP.

All three systems sit on the same physical network. Using the same PTP domain for all three would cause problems.

The chaotic scenario:

Line devices might mistakenly sync to the monitoring system's NTP-referenced time (if that NTP device also supports PTP). The low-precision monitoring time "pollutes" the production lines' high-precision sync.

Or, the BMCA might elect a monitoring-system device as the master clock, degrading the production lines' accuracy.

The solution: use different domains.

- Line A: domainNumber = 0
- Line B: domainNumber = 1
- Monitoring system: no PTP, or domainNumber = 2

The PTP message header carries the domainNumber; devices only process messages whose domainNumber matches their own. Messages of different domains don't interfere.

### Domain identifiers: domainNumber and sdoId

PTP uses two fields to identify a domain:

#### domainNumber: the primary domain identifier

- Type: UInteger8 (0-255)
- Role: the primary, user-configurable isolation mechanism
- Default: 0

Allocation rules:

| domainNumber range | Purpose |
|:---|:---|
| **0-127** | User-defined (the default) |
| **128-239** | Special purposes (partially reserved) |
| **240-255** | Reserved, must not be used |

Note: different transports restrict the domainNumber range. For example, PTP over IEEE 802.3 (Layer 2 Ethernet) allows 0-127, while PTP over UDP/IPv4/IPv6 (Layer 3) permits a larger range.

#### sdoId: standards-organization isolation

- Type: 12-bit integer (0-4095)
- Role: isolates profiles defined by different standards organizations
- Structure: high 4 bits = majorSdoId, low 8 bits = minorSdoId

sdoId exists because different standards organizations may define different PTP profiles (configuration rules). For example:

- IEEE 802.1 defines 802.1AS (audio-video bridging)
- ITU-T defines G.8275.1 (telecom time synchronization)
- IEC defines IEC 62439-3 (industrial networks)

These profiles have different defaults and different options. If they used the same domainNumber, devices of different profiles could interfere with each other.

sdoId allocation:

| sdoId range | Purpose | Administered by |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **0x000** | Default (no special profile) |: |
| **0x100** | IEEE 802.1 profile | IEEE 802.1 Working Group |
| **0x200** | Common Mean Link Delay Service | IEEE 1588 Working Group |
| **0x300-0xFFC** | QSDO-registered profiles | Apply to IEEE RA |
| **0xFFD, 0xFFE** | Experimental use | Temporary |
| **0xFFF** | IEEE 1588 Working Group reserved | IEEE 1588 Working Group |

QSDO stands for Qualified Standards Development Organization.

To become a QSDO, an organization must:
- Have members from multiple companies or academic/government entities.
- Not be dominated by a single entity.
- Have its profiles approved through member voting or an official process.

A QSDO may apply to IEEE RA for a unique sdoId identifying its profiles.

### The practical effect of domain isolation

Scenario: a network running both IEEE 802.1AS and ITU-T G.8275.1.

- IEEE 802.1AS devices: domainNumber = 0, sdoId = 0x100
- ITU-T G.8275.1 devices: domainNumber = 0, sdoId = 0x300 (assuming ITU-T has been assigned this value)

Result:

When an 802.1AS device receives a G.8275.1 device's Announce:

1. Check domainNumber: both 0, matches.
2. Check majorSdoId: 0x100 vs 0x300, mismatch.
3. Drop the message, don't process it.

And vice versa: the G.8275.1 device drops the 802.1AS device's messages.

The two PTP networks operate independently on the same physical network, without interference.

## Timescale: time's "time zone"

### Two timescales

PTP supports two timescales:

#### The PTP Timescale

Definition:
- Epoch: 1 January 1970 00:00:00 TAI
- Second length: the TAI second (the SI second, defined by international atomic time)
- Traceability: traceable to international standards

Key concept: TAI (International Atomic Time)

TAI = International Atomic Time, maintained by the BIPM (International Bureau of Weights and Measures).

TAI is based on the average of roughly 400 atomic clocks worldwide, using the SI second (defined by the cesium-133 hyperfine transition) as the time unit.

TAI's properties:
- Stable: doesn't vary with the Earth's rotation.
- Uniform: no leap-second jumps.
- Traceable: can be traced to international standards.

The relationship between PTP time and TAI:

PTP time = TAI time

Exactly. The PTP epoch is precisely 1 January 1970 00:00:00 TAI.

#### The ARB timescale (Arbitrary Timescale)

Definition:
- Epoch: set by an administrative process
- Second length: determined by the master clock
- Traceability: cannot be traced to international standards

Typical scenarios:

- Industrial control: devices only need internal consistency, not alignment with external time.
- Laboratory testing: testing PTP functions, no need for real time.
- Closed networks: isolated from the outside world; time can be set arbitrarily.

How ARB is used:

The master simply says: "The time is now 1000 seconds." All slaves follow and also believe it's 1000 seconds.

What does "1000 seconds" mean? It doesn't matter, as long as everyone agrees it's 1000 seconds, synchronization works.

Note: under the ARB timescale, currentUtcOffset is meaningless, because ARB time cannot be converted to UTC.

### PTP time vs. UTC time: the key differences

UTC (Coordinated Universal Time):
- Built on TAI, but leap seconds are inserted to stay aligned with the Earth's rotation.
- UTC = TAI − accumulated leap seconds.
- As of 2026, the accumulated leap seconds = 37.

PTP time:
- Simply TAI.
- Never jumps due to leap seconds.
- PTP time = UTC + accumulated leap seconds.

Example:

At some instant:
- UTC time: 2026-01-01 00:00:00
- PTP time: 2026-01-01 00:00:37 (37 seconds ahead)

PTP avoids UTC for two reasons.

Reason 1: avoid leap-second jumps

UTC inserts leap seconds. When one occurs, UTC jumps from 23:59:59 to 23:59:60, then to 00:00:00.

For a synchronization system, such a jump is catastrophic. If the master and slave handle the leap second differently, the clocks can suddenly differ by a second.

PTP time never inserts leap seconds and never jumps, stability is better.

Reason 2: time-interval computation

If you measure an interval in UTC, a leap second breaks the math.

Suppose you start timing at 23:59:58 and end at 00:00:01. Normally the interval should be 3 seconds. But if a leap second slipped in (23:59:60), the actual interval is 4 seconds, yet the clocks show only 59→60→00→01, and the computed interval looks like 3 seconds.

PTP time has no leap seconds, so interval computations are always exact.

### Computing UTC from PTP time: currentUtcOffset

A PTP device learns UTC through the currentUtcOffset field.

currentUtcOffset:
- Definition: the current value of TAI − UTC
- Units: seconds
- Source: IERS Bulletin C (the bulletin of leap seconds published by the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service)

How it works:

A master clock (usually GPS-fed) extracts the current accumulated leap seconds from the GPS signal and writes it into the currentUtcOffset field of its Announce messages.

Once a slave receives the Announce, it knows:

```
UTC time = PTP time − currentUtcOffset
```

Example:

```
PTP time: 2026-01-01 00:00:37
currentUtcOffset: 37
UTC time = 00:00:37 − 37 = 00:00:00
```

### Leap-second notice: leap59 and leap61

Leap seconds are announced in advance; they don't happen suddenly.

IERS publishes notices months ahead: "A positive leap second will be inserted at such-and-such a date."

PTP conveys the notice through two flags:

#### leap61: the positive-leap-second flag

- Meaning: the last minute of the month will have 61 seconds (a leap second will be inserted)
- Value: TRUE or FALSE

#### leap59: the negative-leap-second flag

- Meaning: the last minute of the month will have 59 seconds (a leap second will be deleted)
- Value: TRUE or FALSE

Note: to date, no negative leap second has ever occurred. The Earth's rotation has been slowing, so only positive leap seconds have been inserted.

What PTP does at a leap second:

Master clock (GPS-fed):
1. GPS signals announce the leap second in advance.
2. The master sets leap61=TRUE in Announces before the leap second.
3. After the leap second, currentUtcOffset increments by one.

Slave clocks:
1. Receive the Announce with leap61=TRUE.
2. Know a leap second is coming, prepare to adjust the UTC computation.
3. After the leap second, compute UTC with the new currentUtcOffset.

The key point: PTP time itself never changes; only the UTC computation changes.

## Traceability information: time's "visa"

### What is traceability?

Definition: the property of a measurement or standard value, whereby it is related to a stated reference through a documented, unbroken chain of comparisons, all with stated uncertainties.

In plain terms: where did your time come from? Is the source reliable? Can it be traced to an international standard?

### The traceability flags

PTP defines two traceability flags:

#### timeTraceable: the time is traceable

- Meaning: the master's time can be traced to an international standard (such as UTC)
- Typical sources: GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, national time laboratories

Example:

A master clock has a GPS antenna. GPS time is traceable to the U.S. Naval Observatory (USNO), and USNO's time is traceable to International Atomic Time (TAI).

The chain is continuous and documented, so the master can declare timeTraceable=TRUE.

#### frequencyTraceable: the frequency is traceable

- Meaning: the master's frequency can be traced to an international standard
- Typical sources: Synchronous Ethernet (SyncE), rubidium and cesium atomic clocks

Example:

A master clock obtains frequency from Synchronous Ethernet. SyncE's frequency comes from the telecom operator's network, which is disciplined by a cesium clock, whose frequency traces to the SI definition of the second.

Therefore the master can declare frequencyTraceable=TRUE.

### timeSource: the type of time source

The Announce message carries a timeSource field, indicating which kind of source the master's time comes from.

Enumeration values:

| Value (hex) | Time source | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **0x10** | ATOMIC_CLOCK | Atomic clock (cesium, rubidium) |
| **0x20** | GNSS | Global navigation satellite systems (GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou) |
| **0x30** | TERRESTRIAL_RADIO | Terrestrial radio time service (e.g., WWVB, BPC) |
| **0x40** | PTP | Time obtained from another PTP domain |
| **0x50** | NTP | Time obtained from an NTP server |
| **0x60** | HAND_SET | Set by hand |
| **0x90** | OTHER | Other |
| **0xA0** | INTERNAL_OSCILLATOR | Internal oscillator |

What timeSource does:

- Informational: doesn't participate in BMCA decisions.
- Diagnostic: helps administrators understand the master's provenance.
- Auditing: used for time-traceability audits.

## A complete example of passing time information

A worked example shows how time information flows through a PTP network.

Topology:

```
GPS satellite
  ↓
Master clock A (GPS antenna + rubidium clock)
  ↓
Boundary clock B
  ↓
Slave C (5G base station)
```

Master clock A's data:

| Field | Value | Notes |
|:---|:---|:---|
| clockClass | 6 | synchronized directly to GPS |
| clockAccuracy | 0x20 | accuracy ≤ 25 ns |
| ptpTimescale | TRUE | uses the PTP timescale |
| timeTraceable | TRUE | time traceable to GPS |
| frequencyTraceable | TRUE | frequency traceable to the rubidium clock |
| timeSource | 0x20 (GNSS) | time comes from GPS |
| currentUtcOffset | 37 | TAI−UTC = 37 seconds |
| leap61 | FALSE | no leap second this month |
| leap59 | FALSE | no negative leap second |

The Announce message:

Master A sends Announce messages carrying all the above.

Boundary clock B's handling:

1. Receive the Announce.
2. Extract the time information.
3. Update its own data sets:
   - parentDS.grandmasterIdentity = A's clockIdentity
   - parentDS.grandmasterClockQuality = A's clockQuality
   - timePropertiesDS.currentUtcOffset = 37
   - timePropertiesDS.timeTraceable = TRUE
   - and so on.
4. Forward the Announce (updating some fields, such as stepsRemoved + 1).

Slave C's reception:

1. Receive BC B's forwarded Announce.
2. Extract the time information.
3. Update its own data sets.

Slave C obtains UTC from its local PTP time.

Slave C's local PTP time (obtained through synchronization):

```
PTP time = 2026-01-01 00:00:37 (master A's time)
```

From the Announce:

```
currentUtcOffset = 37
```

Computing UTC:

```
UTC time = PTP time − currentUtcOffset
UTC time = 2026-01-01 00:00:37 − 37 = 2026-01-01 00:00:00
```

In slave C's application layer:

The 5G base station's applications need UTC (for log timestamps, scheduling, etc.).

Inside the base station:
- The PTP stack maintains PTP time.
- When an application API requests time, the stack automatically subtracts currentUtcOffset and returns UTC.

## Practical problems in timescale conversion

### Problem 1: GPS time vs. UTC time

Background:

GPS time is also a continuous timescale with no leap seconds. But its epoch differs from the PTP epoch.

- GPS epoch: 6 January 1980 00:00:00 UTC
- PTP epoch: 1 January 1970 00:00:00 TAI

GPS receivers typically output either GPS time or UTC time (GPS time minus accumulated leap seconds).

Conversion:

If a master clock is GPS-fed, it must know what the receiver is outputting:

- Outputs GPS time: add the TAI−GPS offset (always 19 s) to get TAI, i.e., PTP time.
- Outputs UTC time: add the accumulated leap seconds (37 s as of 2026) to get PTP time.

Configuration example:

```bash
# Master clock config (assuming the GPS receiver outputs UTC)
ptp timescale ptp
ptp current-utc-offset 37  # set the current leap-second value
ptp time-traceable true
ptp frequency-traceable true
ptp time-source gnss
```

### Problem 2: NTP time vs. PTP time

Background:

NTP (Network Time Protocol) is the most widely used time-sync protocol on the Internet.

NTP time:
- Epoch: 1 January 1900 00:00:00 UTC
- Representation: 32-bit seconds + 32-bit fractional seconds
- Rollover: wraps roughly every 136 years (rolls over in 2036)

The problem:

If a PTP master obtains time from an NTP server, be aware:

1. NTP time typically carries millisecond-level error, it cannot provide nanosecond accuracy.
2. NTP time is usually UTC, requiring conversion to PTP time.
3. NTP leap-second handling may differ from PTP's.

Recommendations:

- For nanosecond accuracy, don't use NTP as the time source.
- For millisecond accuracy, NTP is acceptable, but handle the time conversion correctly.

### Problem 3: inconsistent leap-second handling

Background:

When a leap second occurs, if devices in the network handle it inconsistently, time can fall into chaos.

Common problems:

- The master handles the leap second correctly, but some slaves don't.
- Different devices handle it at different times (some early, some late).

Solutions:

1. Use the PTP timescale: PTP time skips leap seconds, avoiding jumps.
2. Configure currentUtcOffset correctly: the master updates it promptly.
3. Watch leap61/leap59: slaves learn about leap seconds in advance.
4. Test and verify: simulate before a real leap second.

## Summary: the core points of domains and timescales

Domain:
- Isolates different PTP networks.
- Identified doubly by domainNumber and sdoId.
- Messages in different domains don't interfere.

Timescale:
- PTP timescale: TAI, traceable, stable.
- ARB timescale: arbitrary time, not traceable, for closed systems.
- PTP time = UTC + currentUtcOffset.

Leap-second handling:
- PTP time never inserts leap seconds, avoiding jumps.
- leap61/leap59 announce upcoming leap seconds.
- currentUtcOffset computes UTC.

Traceability:
- timeTraceable: whether time is traceable to an international standard.
- frequencyTraceable: whether frequency is traceable.
- timeSource: the type of the time source.

Domains and timescales together define how PTP organizes its "time countries" and "time zones".
