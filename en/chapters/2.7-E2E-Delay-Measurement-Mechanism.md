# 2.7 The Magic of Four Timestamps: The E2E Delay Measurement Mechanism

## A deceptively simple question

Suppose you want to measure how long a tennis ball takes to fly from your hand to a wall and bounce back.

The simplest method:
1. Look at your watch and record the serve time: 10:00:00.000.
2. The ball bounces back; look at your watch and record the catch time: 10:00:02.500.
3. Round-trip time = 2.500 seconds.

But PTP faces a much harder problem.

Problem 1: the master and slave clocks disagree. You use your watch, I use mine; they may differ by seconds or even minutes.

Problem 2: the round-trip path is asymmetric. The ball's outgoing and returning speeds may differ.

Problem 3: we cannot measure the "one-way time" directly, only the round-trip time.

PTP solves these problems with four timestamps.

## The basic principle of the E2E mechanism

### The message exchange

The E2E (end-to-end) delay measurement mechanism involves three message types:

1. Sync: sent by the master, carrying the send timestamp.
2. Delay_Req: sent by the slave, requesting measurement.
3. Delay_Resp: replied by the master, carrying the receive timestamp.

Message exchange timing:

```
Master clock                                  Slave clock
  |                                            |
  |---------- Sync (t1) ---------------------->| (t2)
  |                                            |
  |<---------- Delay_Req ----------------------| (t3)
  |                                            |
  |---------- Delay_Resp (t4) ---------------->|
  |                                            |
```

The four timestamps:

- t1: when the master sent Sync (in master time).
- t2: when the slave received Sync (in slave time).
- t3: when the slave sent Delay_Req (in slave time).
- t4: when the master received Delay_Req (in master time).

### The key insight

The four timestamps fall into a pattern:

- t1 and t4: recorded by the master, in the master's time base.
- t2 and t3: recorded by the slave, in the slave's time base.

The problem: the master and slave time bases differ.

Suppose the slave is 100 ns ahead of the master (offset = +100 ns).

Then:
- What the slave calls "1000 seconds" is really "999.9999999 seconds" in master time.
- Both t2 and t3 recorded by the slave are 100 ns ahead of the master's "true time."

How do we remove the effect of this time deviation?

That's the clever part of the PTP algorithm.

## The derivation: from four timestamps to two key values

### Definitions

Let:
- offset: the slave's time deviation from the master.
  - offset > 0: the slave is ahead of the master.
  - offset < 0: the slave is behind the master.
- delay: the one-way propagation delay (assumed symmetric, i.e., master→slave = slave→master).

### The time relationships

The Sync propagation:

```
The master sends Sync at real time T1; the master reads t1.
After `delay`, the Sync reaches the slave.
At real time T1 + delay the slave receives it and reads t2.
```

Because the slave is `offset` ahead of the master:

```
slave reading = master reading + offset
```

Therefore:

```
t2 = t1 + delay + offset  ... (1)
```

The Delay_Req propagation:

```
The slave sends Delay_Req at real time T3; the slave reads t3.
After `delay`, the Delay_Req reaches the master.
At real time T3 + delay the master receives it and reads t4.
```

Accounting for the offset:

```
t3 = t4 − delay + offset  ... (2)
```

### Solving

We have two equations and two unknowns (delay and offset).

From (1):

```
t2 − t1 = delay + offset
offset = t2 − t1 − delay  ... (1a)
```

From (2):

```
t3 − t4 = −delay + offset
offset = t3 − t4 + delay  ... (2a)
```

Combining: set (1a) = (2a):

```
t2 − t1 − delay = t3 − t4 + delay
```

Solve:

```
t2 − t1 − t3 + t4 = 2 × delay
delay = [(t2 − t1) + (t4 − t3)] / 2  ... (3)
```

This is the nominal-value formula for meanPathDelay.

Substitute to find offset:

Put delay into (1a):

```
offset = t2 − t1 − [(t2 − t1) + (t4 − t3)] / 2
       = [(t2 − t1) − (t4 − t3)] / 2  ... (4)
```

This is the nominal value of offsetFromMaster under those assumptions.

## A complete numerical example

A concrete example shows the whole computation.

### The setup

Assumptions:
- True propagation delay between master and slave: 100 ns (symmetric).
- The slave is 50 ns ahead of the master (offset = +50 ns).

The master sends Sync at 1000.000000000 s:

- t1 = 1000.000000000 s (master reading).
- The Sync reaches the slave after 100 ns.
- The slave receives it at real time 1000.000000100 s.
- Slave reading = 1000.000000100 + 50 ns = 1000.000000150 s.
- t2 = 1000.000000150 s.

The slave sends Delay_Req at 1000.001000000 s:

- t3 = 1000.001000000 s (slave reading).
- Real time = 1000.001000000 − 50 ns = 1000.000999950 s.
- The Delay_Req reaches the master after 100 ns.
- The master receives it at real time 1000.001000050 s.
- Master reading = 1000.001000050 s.
- t4 = 1000.001000050 s.

### The computation

The four timestamps:

- t1 = 1000.000000000 s
- t2 = 1000.000000150 s
- t3 = 1000.001000000 s
- t4 = 1000.001000050 s

Compute meanPathDelay:

```
meanPathDelay = [(t2 − t1) + (t4 − t3)] / 2
              = [(150 ns) + (50 ns)] / 2
              = 200 ns / 2
              = 100 ns
```

Correct! The true delay really is 100 ns.

Compute offset:

```
offset = [(t2 − t1) − (t4 − t3)] / 2
       = [150 ns − 50 ns] / 2
       = 100 ns / 2
       = 50 ns
```

Correct! The slave really is 50 ns ahead.

### Verification

Verify the result.

Suppose the slave adjusts its time by the offset:

The slave knows offset = +50 ns and subtracts 50 ns from its time.

The adjusted slave time:

- Original reading: 1000.000000150 s
- Subtract the offset: 1000.000000150 − 50 ns = 1000.000000100 s

Compare with the master:

- Real time the master sent Sync: 1000.000000000 s
- 100 ns after the master sent, the slave receives it.
- Adjusted slave time: 1000.000000100 s.
- The master's true time at that instant: 1000.000000100 s.

Identical! Synchronization succeeds.

## In practice: accounting for the correctionField

The derivation above assumes:
1. Perfectly symmetric propagation.
2. No transparent clocks.
3. No other delays.

In reality, PTP messages may pass through transparent clocks, accumulating the correctionField.

### The complete formulas

meanPathDelay:

```
meanPathDelay = [(t2 − t1) + (t4 − t3)
                 − correctedSyncCorrectionField
                 − Delay_Resp.correctionField] / 2
```

That's the **one-step** form. For **two-step**, also subtract **Follow_Up.correctionField** (and use the Follow_Up's preciseOriginTimestamp instead of the Sync's originTimestamp):

```
meanPathDelay = [(t2 − t1) + (t4 − t3)
                 − correctedSyncCorrectionField
                 − Follow_Up.correctionField
                 − Delay_Resp.correctionField] / 2
```

Where:
- correctedSyncCorrectionField = Sync.correctionField + ingressDelayAsymmetry.

offsetFromMaster:

One-step mode:

```
offsetFromMaster = t2 − originTimestamp − meanPathDelay − correctedSyncCorrectionField
```

Two-step mode:

```
offsetFromMaster = t2 − preciseOriginTimestamp − meanPathDelay
                   − correctedSyncCorrectionField − Follow_Up.correctionField
```

### What the correctionFields contain

Sync.correctionField contains:
- the master clock's fractional-nanosecond part (the part originTimestamp can't represent),
- all transparent clocks' residence times,
- all transparent clocks' ingress asymmetry corrections.

Follow_Up.correctionField contains:
- the precise timestamp supplement in two-step mode,
- corrections accumulated by transparent clocks.

Delay_Resp.correctionField contains:
- the residence times of transparent clocks on the Delay_Req path,
- the egress asymmetry corrections of transparent clocks on the Delay_Req path.

## The effect of asymmetry

### The symmetry assumption

The E2E mechanism's core assumption: master→slave delay = slave→master delay.

If this holds, meanPathDelay is computed accurately.

### What if it's asymmetric?

Suppose:
- Master→slave delay: 100 ns
- Slave→master delay: 150 ns
- True mean delay: 125 ns
- Asymmetry: 25 ns

Compute meanPathDelay:

```
meanPathDelay = [(t2 − t1) + (t4 − t3)] / 2
              = [master→slave delay + slave→master delay] / 2
              = [100 ns + 150 ns] / 2
              = 125 ns
```

meanPathDelay is correct!

But the offset computation goes wrong:

```
offset = [(t2 − t1) − (t4 − t3)] / 2
       = [master→slave delay − slave→master delay] / 2
       = [100 ns − 150 ns] / 2
       = −25 ns
```

Problem: this −25 ns is an error introduced by asymmetry, and it piles on top of the true offset.

### Quantifying the asymmetry error

Let:
- t_ms = master→slave delay
- t_sm = slave→master delay
- true asymmetry = delayAsymmetry = (t_ms − t_sm) / 2

Then:

```
computed offset = true offset + delayAsymmetry
```

Example:

Suppose:
- true offset = +50 ns
- t_ms = 100 ns, t_sm = 150 ns
- delayAsymmetry = (100 − 150) / 2 = −25 ns

Compute:

```
computed offset = 50 ns + (−25 ns) = 25 ns
```

Error = 25 ns. The slave believes it is 25 ns fast; in fact it is 50 ns fast.

### How to cope with asymmetry?

Method 1: measure and configure the asymmetry

If the link's asymmetry is known (e.g., an optical-fiber length difference), configure `portDS.delayAsymmetry` through a management interface.

PTP compensates this value during computation.

Method 2: use the P2P mechanism

The P2P mechanism measures delay link by link, giving better control over asymmetry. But P2P has its own asymmetry issues (see the next section).

Method 3: external calibration

Use external instruments (oscilloscope, time-interval counter) to measure the actual asymmetry, then configure it into the PTP device.

Note: the standard doesn't specify which instrument or measurement method. It only says that when you take the "configure portDS.delayAsymmetry" route, the value is "based, for example, on external measurement (**outside the scope of the standard**) and configured through a management mechanism." The instruments listed above are engineering practice, not standard clauses.

## When to send Delay_Req

### Two timing options

The standard gives two choices for the **send instants** of consecutive Delay_Reqs.

#### Option 1: randomized send times

The slave sends Delay_Req at the **average** interval advertised by the master in its Delay_Resp (the logMessageInterval field), but the gap between two consecutive messages comes from a **random distribution**, not a fixed period.

Advantages:
- Simple.
- The average rate is controlled; randomizing the gap keeps multiple slaves from colliding at the same point in every period.

Disadvantages:
- Wastes bandwidth (even when no delay measurement is needed).
- A single gap is unpredictable, so the update instant of a given delay measurement can't be scheduled ahead of time.

#### Option 2: send as soon as a Sync arrives

The slave sends Delay_Req as soon as it receives a Sync.

Advantages:
- Updates the delay measurement promptly.
- Reduces latency (Sync and Delay_Req are close together).

Disadvantages:
- **Not allowed** with multicast by default, unless a profile says otherwise.
- May increase network traffic bursts.

### What the PTP standard requires

The standard says:

- The **arithmetic mean** of Delay_Req send intervals should be ≥ 0.9 × 2^(logMinDelayReqInterval).
- A single interval may be chosen at random: by default it is uniformly distributed in [0, 2^(logMinDelayReqInterval+1)) seconds, so a single interval can come close to 0.
- logMinDelayReqInterval does not come from the master's Announce. It comes from the **logMessageInterval** field of the **last Delay_Resp received**.

Example:

If the last Delay_Resp received has logMessageInterval = 0 (a mean interval of 1 s), the slave's mean Delay_Req interval should be ≥ 0.9 s, but a single interval can fall anywhere in [0, 2) seconds, as low as nearly 0.

## A complete E2E measurement walkthrough

### Scenario

```
Master A ---- TC_B ---- Slave C
```

Parameters:
- Master A sends Sync at t1 = 1000.000000000 s
- A↔TC_B propagation: 40 ns; TC_B↔C propagation: 40 ns (80 ns one-way total, symmetric)
- TC_B residence time: 5 µs
- Slave C's offset from master A: 0 (to make verification easy)

### The message exchange

Step 1: master A sends Sync

- originTimestamp = 1000.000000000 s
- correctionField = 0

Step 2: the Sync passes through TC_B

- TC_B measures the residence time: 5 µs
- Updates correctionField: 0 + 5 µs = 5 µs

Step 3: slave C receives the Sync

- Receive timestamp: t2 = 1000.000005080 s
  (= send time + 40 ns + 5 µs residence + 40 ns)
- Sync.correctionField = 5 µs

Step 4: slave C sends Delay_Req

- Send timestamp: t3 = 1000.010000000 s
- Delay_Req.correctionField = 0

Step 5: the Delay_Req passes through TC_B

- TC_B measures the residence time: 5 µs
- Updates correctionField: 0 + 5 µs = 5 µs

Step 6: master A receives the Delay_Req

- Receive timestamp: t4 = 1000.010005080 s
- Delay_Req.correctionField = 5 µs

Step 7: master A sends Delay_Resp

- receiveTimestamp = t4 = 1000.010005080 s
- Delay_Resp.correctionField = 5 µs (it copies the correction accumulated on the Delay_Req path. Delay_Resp is a general message: with the E2E TC's egress **one-step** option, the TC doesn't modify its correctionField; if the TC uses egress **two-step**, it adds the Delay_Req's residence time to Delay_Resp.correctionField)

Step 8: slave C receives the Delay_Resp

- Delay_Resp.receiveTimestamp = 1000.010005080 s
- Delay_Resp.correctionField = 5 µs

### Computation

The four timestamps:

- t1 = 1000.000000000 s
- t2 = 1000.000005080 s
- t3 = 1000.010000000 s
- t4 = 1000.010005080 s

Correction fields:

- Sync.correctionField = 5 µs
- Delay_Resp.correctionField = 5 µs
- ingressDelayAsymmetry = 0
- correctedSyncCorrectionField = 5 µs + 0 = 5 µs

Compute meanPathDelay:

```
meanPathDelay = [(t2 − t1) + (t4 − t3)
                 − correctedSyncCorrectionField
                 − Delay_Resp.correctionField] / 2
              = [(5080 ns) + (5080 ns) − 5000 ns − 5000 ns] / 2
              = 160 ns / 2
              = 80 ns
```

The result is 80 ns, exactly the true one-way link delay from A to C (40 ns + 40 ns); the residence time has been deducted through the correctionField.

Compute offset (one-step):

```
offsetFromMaster = t2 − t1 − meanPathDelay − correctedSyncCorrectionField
                 = 5080 ns − 80 ns − 5000 ns
                 = 0 ns
```

The result is 0 ns, matching the assumption (C is already synchronized to A).

## The E2E mechanism's pros and cons

### Advantages

1. End-to-end measurement

It directly measures the whole path from master to slave, requiring no intermediate device to support the P2P mechanism.

2. Simple

Only three message types (Sync, Delay_Req, Delay_Resp) are involved; implementation is relatively easy.

3. Compatible with transparent clocks

Transparent clocks correctly handle Sync and Delay_Req, accumulating residence times.

4. Suits hierarchical networks

In networks with multiple tiers of boundary clocks, E2E works correctly.

### Disadvantages

1. Depends on symmetry

It assumes symmetric round-trip delay; asymmetry introduces error.

2. Larger measurement latency

One-step needs three message types (Sync + Delay_Req + Delay_Resp); two-step sends an extra Follow_Up, four in all, so the measurement latency is larger.

3. Many messages

Every slave must send Delay_Req; in a large network, message volume can be significant.

4. Heavy master load

The master must respond to every slave's Delay_Req; the load can be heavy.

## Summary: the E2E mechanism's core points

Basic principle:
- The master sends Sync, recording t1.
- The slave receives Sync, recording t2.
- The slave sends Delay_Req, recording t3.
- The master receives Delay_Req, recording t4.
- The slave receives Delay_Resp and obtains t4.

Core formulas (nominal values):

```
meanPathDelay = [(t2 − t1) + (t4 − t3)] / 2

nominal offsetFromMaster = [(t2 − t1) − (t4 − t3)] / 2
```

The offset expression holds only when the path is symmetric and no transparent clock contributes a correction.

Key assumptions:
- Symmetric round-trip delay.
- The master and slave frequencies agree (or nearly so).

Effect of asymmetry:
- meanPathDelay is unaffected.
- offsetFromMaster picks up an error = delayAsymmetry.

In practice:
- The correctionField must be accounted for.
- Transparent clocks' residence times must be accounted for.
- Asymmetry corrections must be accounted for.

## Coming up next

Now we know how the E2E mechanism uses four timestamps to measure path delay.

But E2E rests on one key assumption: symmetric round-trip delay. If the path is asymmetric, it introduces error.

More importantly, E2E measures the delay over the **whole path**. It can't see the delay of each intermediate link.

Is there a mechanism that measures delay **link by link** and handles asymmetry better?

The answer: the **P2P mechanism**.

The next section digs into P2P delay measurement: how Pdelay_Req/Pdelay_Resp measure delay link by link.
